Physical Pharmaceutics I syllabus (BP302T), unit-wise

  1. Unit 1 · Solubility of drugs

    Solubility expressions, solute–solvent interactions, ideal solubility parameters, solvation and association, factors influencing solubility, diffusion in biological systems. Solubility of gases in liquids and of liquids in liquids; Raoult's law, ideal and real solutions; partially miscible liquids, critical solution temperature; distribution law, its limitations and applications.

  2. Unit 2 · States of matter & physicochemical properties

    Changes in state, latent heat, vapour pressure, sublimation, critical point, eutectic mixtures, gases, aerosols (inhalers), relative humidity, liquid crystals, glassy state, crystalline and amorphous solids, polymorphism. Refractive index, optical rotation, dielectric constant, dipole moment, dissociation constant: determination and applications.

  3. Unit 3 · Surface and interfacial phenomenon

    Liquid interfaces, surface and interfacial tension, surface free energy and its measurement, spreading coefficient, adsorption at liquid interfaces, surface active agents, HLB scale, solubilisation, detergency, adsorption at solid interfaces.

  4. Unit 4 · Complexation and protein binding

    Classification of complexes, applications, methods of analysis, protein binding, complexation and drug action, crystalline structure of complexes, thermodynamic treatment of stability constants.

  5. Unit 5 · pH, buffers and isotonic solutions

    Sorensen's pH scale, pH determination (electrometric and colorimetric), buffer equation, buffer capacity, applications of buffers, buffers in pharmaceutical and biological systems, buffered isotonic solutions.

Physical Pharmaceutics I MCQ with answers

Q1. According to the pharmacopoeia, a drug that needs 30 to 100 parts of solvent to dissolve 1 part is described as:

  1. Sparingly soluble
  2. Soluble
  3. Slightly soluble
  4. Very slightly soluble
Show answer

Answer: (a) Sparingly soluble. Sparingly soluble = 30-100 parts; soluble = 10-30; slightly soluble = 100-1000.

Q2. Ideal solubility of a solid in mole fraction depends on:

  1. Dielectric constant of solvent
  2. Viscosity of solvent
  3. Heat of fusion and melting point of the solute
  4. Dipole moment of solvent
Show answer

Answer: (c) Heat of fusion and melting point of the solute. log X2 = -(ΔHf/2.303R)(T0 - T)/(T T0); the solvent does not appear.

Q3. The latent heat of vaporization of water at 100°C is about:

  1. 80 cal/g
  2. 334 cal/g
  3. 1000 cal/g
  4. 540 cal/g
Show answer

Answer: (d) 540 cal/g. About 540 cal/g (2257 J/g); 80 cal/g is the latent heat of fusion of ice.

Q4. A plot of log vapour pressure against 1/T gives a straight line with slope equal to:

  1. +ΔHv/2.303R
  2. -ΔHv/2.303R
  3. -ΔHf/R
  4. ΔS/R
Show answer

Answer: (b) -ΔHv/2.303R. Clausius-Clapeyron: log p = -ΔHv/(2.303 R T) + constant.

Q5. The surface tension of water at 20°C is about:

  1. 72.8 dyne/cm
  2. 22 dyne/cm
  3. 50 dyne/cm
  4. 100 dyne/cm
Show answer

Answer: (a) 72.8 dyne/cm. Water ≈ 72.8 dyne/cm (mN/m) at 20°C.

Q6. In the capillary rise method (contact angle zero), surface tension is given by:

  1. γ = r h ρ g
  2. γ = 2 r h ρ g
  3. γ = ½ r h ρ g
  4. γ = h ρ g / r
Show answer

Answer: (c) γ = ½ r h ρ g. 2πrγ = πr²hρg, so γ = ½ r h ρ g.

Q7. β-cyclodextrin contains how many glucose units?

  1. 5
  2. 6
  3. 8
  4. 7
Show answer

Answer: (d) 7. α = 6, β = 7, γ = 8 glucose units.

Q8. Quinhydrone is an example of:

  1. Metal chelate
  2. Organic molecular complex
  3. Clathrate
  4. Channel lattice compound
Show answer

Answer: (b) Organic molecular complex. Quinhydrone is a 1:1 molecular (charge-transfer) complex of quinone and hydroquinone.

Q9. Maximum buffer capacity of a buffer of total concentration C is:

  1. 0.576 C
  2. 0.25 C
  3. 0.5 C
  4. 2.303 C
Show answer

Answer: (a) 0.576 C. βmax = 2.303 C/4 = 0.576 C at pH = pKa.

Q10. The pH of a buffer containing equal molar amounts of acetic acid (pKa 4.76) and sodium acetate is:

  1. 3.76
  2. 5.76
  3. 4.76
  4. 7.00
Show answer

Answer: (c) 4.76. pH = pKa + log 1 = pKa.

Sample model answers

How a good exam answer for Physical Pharmaceutics I looks (sample answers; the full set opens in the daily practice).

[2 marks] Define solubility.

Solubility is the concentration of solute in a saturated solution at a definite temperature, i.e. when solute and solvent are in equilibrium.

Pharmacopoeial terms (parts of solvent for 1 part of solute):

Very soluble: less than 1

Freely soluble: 1-10

Soluble: 10-30

Sparingly soluble: 30-100

Slightly soluble: 100-1000

Very slightly soluble: 1000-10,000

Practically insoluble: more than 10,000.

[2 marks] Write the different ways of expressing the concentration (solubility) of a solution.

Solubility/concentration can be expressed as:

1. Molarity (M): moles of solute per litre of solution.

2. Molality (m): moles of solute per 1000 g (1 kg) of solvent; not affected by temperature.

3. Normality (N): gram equivalent weights of solute per litre of solution.

4. Mole fraction: moles of one component divided by total moles of all components.

5. Percentage: % w/w, % w/v and % v/v.

6. Parts of solvent required to dissolve 1 part of solute (pharmacopoeial terms).

[10 marks] Explain the quantitative approach to the various factors influencing the solubility of drugs. State the Nernst Distribution Law and discuss its pharmaceutical applications and limitations.

Solubility is the concentration of solute in a saturated solution at a definite temperature. Several factors influence it in a measurable (quantitative) way; the distribution of a solute between two immiscible phases is described by the Nernst distribution law.

  • Temperature: ln(X2) varies with 1/T (van't Hoff equation); solubility rises with temperature for endothermic (positive heat of solution) solutes and falls for exothermic ones.
  • pH: for a weak acid, total solubility S = S0 (1 + 10^(pH - pKa)); for a weak base, S = S0 (1 + 10^(pKa - pH)), where S0 is solubility of the un-ionised form. The pH below which a weak acid precipitates (pHp) is pHp = pKa + log[(S - S0)/S0].
  • Particle size: very fine particles are more soluble: log(S/S0) = 2γV/(2.303 R T r) (Ostwald-Freundlich equation), where r = particle radius.
  • Molecular structure, polymorphism and crystal form: metastable polymorphs and amorphous forms have lower lattice energy and higher solubility.
  • Co-solvents (ethanol, PEG, propylene glycol) reduce polarity of water: log S of mixture changes roughly linearly with fraction of co-solvent; surfactants above CMC solubilise drugs in micelles; complexing agents form soluble complexes; common ion effect lowers solubility (Ksp).
  • Nernst distribution law: at constant temperature, a solute distributes between two immiscible solvents so that K = C1/C2 is constant (partition or distribution coefficient).
  • Modifications: for association in one phase K = C1^(1/n)/C2; for dissociation only the un-ionised fraction is used.
  • Limitations: valid only at constant temperature, in dilute solutions, with mutually immiscible solvents, when solute is in the same molecular form in both phases and does not react with the solvent.
  • Applications: solvent extraction of drugs (several small extractions are more efficient), partition chromatography, prediction of absorption and distribution (log P), preservative action in emulsions, study of association and complexation.

Diagram: Graph: solubility of a weak acid vs pH showing a flat region (S0) at low pH rising sharply above pKa. Also a separating funnel with organic layer C1 and aqueous layer C2.

Conclusion: Quantitative relations let the formulator predict and improve solubility, and the distribution law links solubility to extraction and drug absorption.
